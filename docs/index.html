Abstract

This study addresses the challenge of identifying which pages should be prioritized for content review or refresh, using content-performance signals such as search impressions, clicks, CTR, and search position.

Initial analysis showed that page activity and search performance were associated with CTR and click volume. We then compared a rule-based scoring approach with Decision Tree and Random Forest models to evaluate whether machine learning could improve page prioritization.

The Random Forest model achieved a Precision@20 of 0.85 (85%), compared with 0.20 (20%) for the baseline rule-based score. 17 of the top 20 ranked pages corresponded to the target priority class, versus only 4 of 20 for the baseline.

These findings suggest that combining multiple content-performance signals through a learned model can improve the identification of pages that may benefit from content review or refresh, supporting SEO teams in prioritizing a limited number of pages for further investigation.

01 · Introduction

The decision this work supports

Websites accumulate hundreds or thousands of content pages over time, but not every page performs equally. Some pages maintain strong search visibility, while others show weak performance or signs that they may need attention.

This work addresses one specific decision: which page should an SEO manager review first, given limited time and resources.

The output is intended to help prioritize pages for content refresh, title or metadata review, or closer monitoring. Reviewing a page that does not need attention wastes limited resources, while missing a genuinely underperforming page may result in lost search opportunities.

The objective of this study is to develop and evaluate a scoring approach that combines multiple content-performance signals to prioritize pages for content review or refresh.
02 · Data

Source, scope, and what was excluded

Development began on a small static CSV from the starter repository, then moved to FlyRank's production data warehouse, hosted on Hugging Face and queried with DuckDB.

The primary table was fact_content_daily_performance, containing daily page-level performance across multiple clients.

Feature availability

The data contract identified a broader set of potential features, including GA4 engagement signals and traffic-source data. An availability check showed valid GA4 data in only 4.2% of rows, versus 36.7% for GSC data.

Because of this gap, the final feature set was built entirely from Google Search Console signals:

  • active_days — distinct tracked days in the window
  • total_impressions
  • total_clicks
  • avg_position
  • ctr
Split Design Because the dataset spans multiple clients, a client-grouped split kept all pages from a given client entirely within either training or test data — reducing client-specific leakage and forcing the model to learn generalizable patterns rather than memorize individual clients.

Records missing information required for a given analysis were excluded so scoring and modeling ran only on usable rows.

03 · Methodology

From validated signals to a ranked score

The methodology was developed in two stages: first validating individual signals against the data, then building a rule-based score and comparing it with learned models.

3.1 · Signal test — Activity

Pages were grouped by tracked activity: low (≤10 days), medium (11–20 days), high (>20 days).

Activity bucket Avg. impressions Avg. clicks
High activity 2,637.4 7.6
Medium activity 283.7 1.2
Low activity 26.9 0.1

Activity was clearly associated with performance, supporting its use as a screening signal — though low activity alone does not prove staleness, since it may also reflect limited exposure or tracking gaps.

3.2 · Signal test — Position & CTR

Restricted to pages with GSC data available and at least 10 impressions:

Position bucket Avg. CTR
Top 10 0.34%
Position 11–20 0.26%
Position 20+ 0.13%

Higher-ranking pages had meaningfully higher CTR — evidence supporting position and click opportunity as review-priority signals.

3.3 · Rule-based priority scoring

The baseline rule scores the gap between a page's expected clicks given its position and its actual clicks:

Expected Clicks = Total Impressions × Expected CTR
Opportunity Gap = Expected Clicks − Total Clicks
Priority Score = Opportunity Gap × 100

A higher score means a larger gap between expected and actual clicks — a greater estimated opportunity. This score is the baseline the learned models are measured against.

3.4 · Machine learning comparison

Two models were trained on the same client-grouped split: a Decision Tree and a Random Forest. Both used the five features above to learn combinations associated with the target priority class.

3.5 · Evaluation

All three approaches were compared using Precision@20 and Precision@50 — the share of the top-K ranked pages that belong to the target priority class.

K=20
Precision@20 was selected to reflect limited review capacity.
The practical objective is not to classify every page perfectly, but to produce a short ranking that gives an SEO team a reliable starting point for manual investigation.

Random Forest clearly outperformed the rule-based baseline on both metrics. At Precision@20, it reached 0.85 — 17 of the top 20 ranked pages matched the target class, versus 4 of 20 for the baseline, a 4.25× improvement.

At Precision@50, Random Forest reached 0.68 against 0.14 for the baseline. Precision naturally decreased as the list widened from 20 to 50, but the model still substantially outperformed the rule.

The Decision Tree also beat the baseline by a wide margin (0.55 vs. 0.20 at Precision@20), suggesting that even a simple, readable model captures signal combinations a fixed rule misses.

05 · Limitations & Honest Framing

What this study can — and can't — claim

Scope Results come from a specific FlyRank warehouse release and analysis period, and may not generalize to other periods, datasets, or platforms.
Proxy Label Priority/decline labels were built as a proxy from performance trends, not manually verified ground truth — model performance should be read against that proxy definition.
Small Samples Pages with very low activity and impressions produced unreliable signals. Three of the top 20 Random Forest picks were wrong, all sharing a pattern of 1 active day and 1–2 impressions, where a CTR of 1.0 is noise rather than evidence of strength.
Privacy Client and page identities are hashed and unrecoverable, so individual recommendations could not be externally validated.
Causality This work identifies association, not causation. It does not explain why a page underperforms and makes no claim about Google's ranking algorithm.

The results should be treated as decision-support signals for editorial review — a starting point for a human reviewer, not a final verdict on any individual page.

With meaningful impression volume behind them, these signals are reliable enough to make these pages strong candidates for content review, intent-alignment checks, or a title/metadata rewrite.

Priority 2 — Review soon

Pages ranked 11–20 score roughly 0.61–0.64, showing the same pattern of meaningful impressions with weak position and CTR, at slightly lower confidence than Priority 1. Review after the top five.

Priority 3 — Monitor

Pages with fewer than 10 impressions were excluded from immediate action because their model scores may be noise-driven. Rather than acting now, these pages should be re-evaluated once more impressions and clicks accumulate.
